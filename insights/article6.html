The Swap Agreement: What to Put in Writing Before You Hand Over the Keys

A swap has no front desk, no contract of adhesion, and no one to call at 2 a.m. What you put in writing before the keys change hands is the entire customer-service department.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The keys changed hands on a Tuesday, in the car park of a supermarket halfway between the two houses, because the owners had never met and the swap had been arranged entirely by message. The agreement, such as it was, was a thread of emails and a confirmation document from the platform. It said who was coming, when, and for how long. It did not say who would pay if the boiler broke, or what would happen if the guests smoked indoors, or who was responsible for the dog. None of those things happened, and the swap was a success. The owners knew, afterwards, that they had been lucky. A swap has no front desk, no contract of adhesion, and no one to call at two in the morning. What you put in writing before the keys change hands is the entire customer-service department.

The home swap is an unusual transaction in one respect: it is a contract between two private households, with no professional intermediary, and the platform that introduces them is not a party to it. The platform provides a confirmation document, a set of guarantees, and a messaging system. It does not provide a contract. The contract, if there is one, is the one the two households write themselves. Most do not write one at all. Most rely on the platform's confirmation and on trust. Trust is a good basis for a holiday. It is a poor basis for a dispute.

What the platform gives you, and what it does not

HomeExchange, the largest platform, provides a confirmation document for each exchange, with the contact details and the addresses of both households, and it requires identity verification — a government identity document and proof of address — before a member can exchange. It offers a damage guarantee of up to $1,000,000, with a $500 deductible, and a cancellation guarantee of up to $120 a night for alternative accommodation, up to $840 a week. Those guarantees are real, and they are the reason the platform can charge $235 a year. They are also, as LegalClarity notes in its review of the platform, subject to terms and conditions, and they do not replace the members' own insurance. A guarantee that requires a claim, documentation and an assessment is not the same as a policy that pays.

The confirmation document is not a contract. It records the agreement. It does not set out the consequences of breaking it. It does not say who pays for a broken appliance, or for a stained carpet, or for a neighbour's complaint about noise. It does not say what happens if one household cancels a week before the exchange, or if one household arrives to find the other household's home in a condition that does not match the photographs. The platform's cancellation guarantee covers the cost of alternative accommodation. It does not cover the holiday that was lost.

What to put in writing

The agreement between the two households should be short, specific and written before the exchange, and it should cover five things. The first is the dates and the people: who is coming, how many, and whether children and pets are included. The second is the condition of the home: a photographic record, taken on the day of the exchange, of the rooms, the appliances and any existing damage, so that a dispute about a scratch has an answer. The third is the money: who pays for cleaning, who pays for utilities, and who pays for breakages, and the threshold at which a breakage becomes the guest's responsibility rather than wear and tear. The fourth is the rules: smoking, parties, the use of the car, the use of the pool, the care of the pets and the plants. The fifth is the emergency contact: a neighbour, a friend, or a property manager who has a key and can be reached if the boiler breaks or the lock jams.

There is a sixth item, and it is the one that matters most in a dispute: the fair market value of the stay. UpCounsel, a legal-services site, notes that barter agreements over $1,000 should be in writing, and that the agreement should state the fair market value of what is being exchanged, because the tax authorities treat a barter as income at fair market value. A two-week villa stay has a fair market value of about $2,100. Writing that number into the agreement does two things: it makes the agreement enforceable, because a court can see what was exchanged, and it makes the tax position honest, because the value is stated rather than hidden. The agreement is a contract. The value is the price of the contract.

The form of the agreement does not matter much. A signed document is best. An exchange of emails, each confirming the terms, is good enough in most jurisdictions. What matters is that the terms exist, in writing, before the keys change hands. An agreement written after a dispute has started is not an agreement. It is evidence.

The handover itself deserves a paragraph of its own. Agree in advance how the keys change hands — a lockbox with a code, a neighbour, or a meeting in person — and agree on a check-in and check-out walkthrough, with photographs taken by both households on arrival and on departure. The photographs are the record that settles a dispute about damage, and they cost nothing to take. Agree also on who pays for the cleaning at the end of the stay, and whether the home should be left as found or professionally cleaned. The cleaning question is the most common source of friction in a swap, because each household has a different idea of what clean means, and the idea is rarely discussed until after the stay.

The counterargument: most swaps work on trust, and the paperwork is for the one percent

The counterargument to all of this is that the paperwork is for the one percent of swaps that go wrong, and that the ninety-nine percent work on trust. That is true, and it is not a reason to skip the paperwork. The paperwork costs an hour. The dispute costs a holiday, a friendship, and possibly a legal bill. The households that write an agreement are not the households that distrust each other. They are the households that have understood that a swap is a transaction between strangers, and that strangers are best dealt with in writing.

There is a second counterargument, and it is about enforcement. An agreement between two households in two countries is enforceable in theory and difficult in practice. A dispute over a $500 carpet stain, between a household in Ireland and a household in Portugal, is not a dispute that any court will hear cheaply, and the cost of pursuing it exceeds the value of the claim. The agreement's real function is not to win in court. It is to prevent the dispute, by making the terms clear before the exchange, and to give the platform a basis for acting if a member behaves badly. The platform can suspend a member. A court cannot, cheaply, make a stranger pay for a carpet.

The honest version of the swap agreement is this: it is a short document, written before the exchange, that states the dates, the people, the condition of the home, the money, the rules, the emergency contact and the fair market value. It takes an hour to write. It is the difference between a holiday and a dispute, and the difference is worth an hour.
