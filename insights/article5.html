How Much a Villa Swap Actually Saves — and What It Costs Instead

A two-week villa stay rents for about $2,100. A swap costs a $235 membership, a cleaning bill, and the flights. The savings are real — and they evaporate exactly where you stop counting.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The villa in the Algarve was listed at €2,400 a week. The family of four, flying in from Dublin for a fortnight, did the arithmetic that every villa renter does: two weeks, €4,800, before flights, before food, before the car. Then a friend mentioned a swap. The family listed their own house — a four-bedroom in a Dublin suburb, with a garden and a garage — and within a month they had an offer from a couple in the Algarve who wanted two weeks in Ireland. The cost of the fortnight, in the end, was a $235 membership, a cleaning bill, and the flights they were buying anyway. The saving was real. It was also not the whole story, because a swap does not cost nothing. It costs something else, and the something else is where the arithmetic gets interesting.

The home-swap proposition is simple enough to state in a sentence: two households exchange homes for a holiday, and neither pays rent. The saving is the rent that is not paid. The cost is everything else — the membership, the cleaning, the preparation, the risk — and the saving is only a saving if the everything else adds up to less than the rent. For a villa holiday, it usually does. For a short stay, it often does not. The difference is the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is worth doing properly, because the platforms advertise the saving and not the cost.

The worked example: two weeks in a villa

Take the worked example from HomeExchange, the largest home-swap platform, with more than 360,000 homes in 150 countries. A fortnight in a large home rents for roughly €1,300 to €2,400 a week, according to the Irish Times, which profiled a family that swapped its Dublin home for a house in Quebec. HomeExchange's own comparison is more direct: six trips totalling 38 nights cost $5,550 in hotels and rentals, against a $235 annual membership. Its 14-night line item is $2,100 for the rental equivalent. The membership, in the United States, is $235 a year, for unlimited exchanges.

Run the numbers for a single two-week villa stay. The rental equivalent is about $2,100. The swap costs $235 for the membership — though the membership covers the whole year, so a family that swaps twice pays about $118 a trip — plus a cleaning bill. LegalClarity, a legal-information site that has examined the swap platforms in detail, puts the cost of cleaning a three-bedroom home between guests at $100 to $150. Add a second cleaning, for the home you are leaving and the home you are returning to, and the cleaning bill is $200 to $300. The flights are the same either way. The total cost of the swap, for a family that swaps twice a year, is roughly $320 to $420. The saving, against a $2,100 rental, is about $1,700. That is a real number, and it is the reason the platforms exist.

The Magdalein family, profiled by HomeExchange, saved about $12,000 on accommodation in 2020, spending 45 nights in swapped homes for a $235 membership — about $3 a night. The number is an outlier, a family that swapped aggressively through a year when travel was cheap and the platform was hungry for members. But the direction is right. The saving scales with the number of nights. A family that swaps for a month saves more than a family that swaps for a weekend, because the membership is fixed and the rent is not.

What it costs instead

The costs that replace the rent are smaller, but they are not zero, and they are the costs the advertising does not mention. The first is the preparation. A home that is to be swapped must be cleaned, and not in the way a home is cleaned for guests. It must be cleaned to the standard of a stranger who is paying nothing and expects a hotel. The linen must be fresh, the kitchen must be stocked with the basics, the personal items must be put away, the valuables must be locked up. The time that takes is a weekend, and the cost, if a cleaner is hired, is $100 to $150 a turnover.

The second cost is the platform's own risk. HomeExchange offers a damage guarantee of up to $1,000,000, with a $500 deductible, and a cancellation guarantee of up to $120 a night for alternative accommodation, up to $840 a week. Those numbers are real, and they are also subject to terms and conditions, and to a claims process that requires documentation. The guarantee is not insurance in the ordinary sense. It is a platform promise, and a platform promise is only as good as the platform's willingness to pay. The prudent swapper keeps their own home insurance in force and tells the insurer about the swap, because a claim denied on the grounds that the home was occupied by a stranger is a claim denied.

The third cost is the one that does not appear on any invoice: the time. Listing a home, writing a profile, answering messages, vetting the other household, agreeing dates, exchanging keys, and cleaning up afterwards takes hours, and the hours are spread over weeks. A rental takes a booking and a payment. A swap takes a negotiation. The saving is the rent. The price is the time, and the time is worth whatever the swapper's time is worth.

The counterargument: the saving assumes your home is swappable

The counterargument to the swap saving is that it assumes something the advertising does not state: that your home is a home someone else wants. A villa in the Algarve is swappable. A four-bedroom house in a Dublin suburb is swappable, because Dublin is a destination. A flat in a suburb that nobody visits is not swappable, or it is swappable only for a flat in another suburb that nobody visits. The swap market is a market, and like every market it prices location. The families who save the most on swaps are the families whose homes are in the places other people want to go. The families whose homes are not are the families who pay the membership and never find a match.

There is a second counterargument, and it is about the quality of the match. A rental is a transaction: you pay, you get the property as described, and if it is not as described, you have a contract and a platform to complain to. A swap is a reciprocal arrangement between two households, and the quality of the stay depends on the other household's standards, which are not the standards of a professional host. The villa may be beautiful and the kitchen may be empty. The house may be clean and the beds may be uncomfortable. The swapper trades the certainty of a professional rental for the saving, and the saving is the price of the uncertainty.

The honest accounting, then, is this. A two-week villa swap saves about $1,700 against the rental equivalent, for a family that swaps twice a year and whose home is in a place people want to visit. The saving is real, and it is large. It is not free. It costs a membership, two cleanings, a weekend of preparation, and a negotiation. For a family that swaps often, and for a villa that rents dearly, it is the best deal in travel. For a family that swaps once, in a home that is hard to match, it is a membership fee and a story about the time they nearly went to the Algarve.
