The Tax Question Nobody Asks About a Villa Swap

The IRS treats barter as income at fair market value — even when no cash changes hands. A villa swap is a barter. Almost nobody reports it. That gap is the story.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The tax return did not mention the holiday. The family had swapped their house in Dublin for a villa in the Algarve for a fortnight, and the holiday had cost them a membership, a cleaning bill and the flights. There was no rent, no invoice, and no payment to report. The return was filed, the holiday was forgotten, and nobody asked a question. That is the position of almost everyone who has ever swapped a home. It is also, on the face of the tax code, the wrong position. The Internal Revenue Service treats barter as income, at fair market value, even when no cash changes hands. A villa swap is a barter. Almost nobody reports it. That gap is the story.

The rule is not obscure. It is in the IRS's own guidance, and it is stated plainly. The question is not whether the rule exists. The question is whether it applies to a fortnight in someone else's villa, and if it does, what it means for the millions of people who have swapped homes and never thought about it.

What the IRS says about barter

IRS Tax Topic 420, on bartering income, states the rule in a paragraph. Bartering is the exchange of goods or services without the use of money, and the fair market value of the goods or services received must be included in the income of both parties, in the year the barter takes place. The income is reported on Schedule C if the barter is part of a trade or business, and on Schedule 1, as other income, if it is not. Publication 525, the IRS's guide to taxable and nontaxable income, covers bartering in the same terms. The rule is general, and it is not limited to businesses. It applies to anyone who barters, and a home exchange is a barter: one household gives the use of its home, and receives the use of another household's home in return.

The IRS's guidance goes further. Barter exchanges — the organisations that facilitate barter transactions — are required to file Form 1099-B, reporting the transactions of their members. The form is the mechanism by which the IRS learns about barter income that is not reported voluntarily. The question for the home-swap platforms is whether they are barter exchanges for this purpose. No published IRS guidance addresses home-exchange platforms specifically, and the platforms' own position is not stated in their public terms. The rule is clear. Its application to a personal home swap is not.

The fair market value is the other half of the question, and it is easier to answer than the legal question. A two-week stay in a villa that rents for $2,100 has a fair market value of about $2,100. The value is the rental equivalent, and the rental equivalent is a number the market produces every day. A taxpayer who reported the swap would report about $2,100 of income, and would pay tax on it at their marginal rate — roughly $460 at a 22 percent marginal rate, before any deductions. The tax is not large. The compliance question is larger than the tax.

The fourteen-day rule, and why it does not obviously apply

There is a rule that villa owners often cite, and it is worth examining, because it is a real rule that does not obviously cover a swap. IRS Publication 527, on residential rental property, contains the fourteen-day rule: if you rent your home for fourteen days or fewer in a year, the rental income is not taxable, and you do not need to report it. The rule is the reason a homeowner can rent their house for a major event — the Augusta rule, named for the Masters golf tournament — and keep the income tax-free. The rule applies to a rental. A swap is not a rental. There is no rent, and there is no tenant. The fourteen-day rule does not, on its face, cover a barter, because a barter is not a rental. A taxpayer who relies on the fourteen-day rule for a swap is relying on an analogy, and the IRS has not endorsed the analogy.

There is a further complication. If the swap is reported as barter income, the taxpayer may also be entitled to deductions — the cleaning, the membership fee, the cost of preparing the home — as expenses incurred in producing the income. The net income, after those deductions, would be smaller than the gross value, and for a single swap the net could be small enough that the tax is negligible. The compliance cost, however, is not negligible: the record-keeping, the valuation, the reporting, and the risk of an audit question about a transaction that the taxpayer's own records describe as a holiday. The tax system is built for transactions with invoices. A swap has no invoice.

The counterargument: the IRS has never pursued a personal home swap

The counterargument to all of this is the practical one, and it is strong. The IRS has never, to public knowledge, pursued a taxpayer for unreported income from a personal home swap. The reason is not that the rule does not apply. The reason is that the IRS has no way of knowing about the swap, no form that reports it, no audit programme that looks for it, and no revenue at stake that would justify the effort. A single swap generates about $460 of tax. An audit costs the IRS more than that. The enforcement gap is not a legal gap. It is a practical gap, and it is the reason the rule is honoured in the breach.

There is a second counterargument, and it is about the economics. A reciprocal swap is, in economic terms, a wash: each household receives a stay worth about what it gives, and each household's income is the other's expense. If both households reported the fair market value, both would pay tax on the same economic event, and the net revenue to the Treasury would be the tax on two stays that were, in substance, a single exchange of hospitality. The tax code taxes barter because barter is a way of hiding income. A home swap is not a way of hiding income. It is a way of taking a holiday without paying rent, and the rent that is not paid is not income in any sense that a taxpayer would recognise.

There is also the question of the platform's own currency. HomeExchange operates a points system, GuestPoints, in which a member earns points by hosting and spends them by staying. The points have a value, and the value is set by the platform. Whether GuestPoints are a barter currency, and whether the earning and spending of them is a taxable event, is a question that no published IRS guidance addresses. The points are, in substance, a record of hospitality exchanged. In form, they are a currency. The tax treatment of a platform currency is a question the IRS has not answered, and the members are left to guess.

What to do, in practice

The practical advice is short. Keep a record of the swap: the dates, the property, the fair market value, and the costs. The record is the answer to any question that is ever asked, and it costs nothing to keep. If the swap is part of a business — if the villa is a rental property, and the swap is a way of filling a gap in the calendar — the income should be reported, because the property is already in the tax system and the swap is a transaction in that business. If the swap is personal, the position of the overwhelming majority of swappers is not to report it, and the risk of that position is, on the evidence, negligible. That is not legal advice. It is a description of what happens, and what does not happen.

The tax question nobody asks about a villa swap is a question about the gap between the rule and the reality. The rule says the swap is income. The reality says nobody reports it, and nobody is asked. The gap will close, if it closes, when the platforms are treated as barter exchanges and required to report, or when the IRS publishes guidance specific to home exchanges. Until then, the honest position is to know the rule, keep the record, and understand that the risk is not the tax. The risk is not knowing the rule exists.
