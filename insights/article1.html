The Villa at the Top of the Swap

Home exchange has grown into a multi-billion-dollar market. Villas are its premium tier — and its hardest inventory to win.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 6 min read

The couple from Lyon did not haggle. They stood on the terrace of a stone villa above Kalkan, looked at the pool dropping toward the sea, and asked the owner one question: what would it take to come back every year? The owner, a retired surgeon from Manchester who had bought the house a decade earlier, had an answer ready. He did not want rent. He wanted a house like his own, somewhere he had not been — a villa in Santorini, a finca in Mallorca, a place with the same quiet and the same standard. That is the trade at the top of the home-exchange market, and it is the trade a villa-only platform exists to make.

Home exchange is no longer a fringe habit of house-proud travelers mailing printed directories to each other. WiseGuy Reports sizes the home-exchange service market at $2.37 billion in 2024, rising to $2.6 billion in 2025 and $6.5 billion by 2035, a compound annual growth rate of 9.6%. A second research house, Dataintelo, puts the market at $1.8 billion in 2025 and $4.2 billion by 2034, a 9.8% annual growth rate, with roughly 180,000 active listings across more than 145 countries. The two houses disagree on the level — research firms always do — and agree on the direction: steady, fast growth, driven by travelers who would rather live in a house than sleep in a hotel room.

The category's center of gravity is HomeExchange, and its history explains why the model works. Ed Kushins founded the original HomeExchange club in 1992 as a mail-order printed directory, after a family swap from Hermosa Beach to Washington, D.C. convinced him the idea could scale. The club moved online in 1996. In 2017 it merged with GuestToGuest, the French platform founded in 2011 by Emmanuel Arnaud and Charles-Édouard Girard, whose GuestPoints system let members host now and travel later instead of matching dates and destinations exactly. The combined company, led by Arnaud as chief executive, kept the HomeExchange name, earned B Corp certification in 2022, and in March 2023 bought its British rival Love Home Swap — founded by Debbie Wosskow — from Travel+Leisure Group. Consolidation made HomeExchange the reference platform of the sector, with a reported 80% share in some accounts of the market.

Scale is the first thing a newcomer must reckon with. HomeExchange lists more than 360,000 homes across 155 countries on its exchange pages, with its strongest presence in the United States, France, and Spain, and charges $235 a year for unlimited exchanges. Its points system has real liquidity: GuestPoints processed more than 2.8 million exchange-night credits in 2024, according to Dataintelo. Points-based exchange, at 26.8% of type revenues, is the fastest-growing segment of the market at a 12.1% annual growth rate through 2034. And the audience is young for a market built on home ownership: millennials and Gen Z together account for nearly 57% of active home-exchange participants as of 2025. The swap is not a retiree hobby. It is how a generation that rents its music and leases its cars thinks about a second home it does not own.

Villas sit at the top of this market for a simple reason: they are where the money already is. Dataintelo sizes the global luxury villa rental market at $19.4 billion in 2025, heading to $38.7 billion by 2034, and notes that beach villas hold the largest property-type share at 38.5% of revenue. The premium tier of exchange already exists inside HomeExchange itself: HomeExchange Collection, launched in late 2021, is a curated community for luxury-home exchange, priced around $1,000 a year against the standard $235. That price gap — four times the standard fee for a vetted luxury pool — is the market telling a founder, in numbers, that villa owners will pay to be separated from the general pool. A villa-only brand does not have to invent the premium tier. It has to give it a front door of its own.

The counterargument is real, and any honest villa-exchange plan must face it. Villas are the hardest inventory in exchange, not just the most valuable. A flat can be swapped on trust and a spare key; a villa has staff rotas, a pool plant room, a wine cellar, art on the walls, and neighbors who notice. Owners worry, reasonably, that a guest who treats a villa like a hotel room will cost more in a week than a year of membership fees. GuestPoints also compress differences: a points value set by size, location, and amenities can still undervalue a truly exceptional house, which is why some owners hold their best weeks back. A villa platform that prices every house on the same grid will lose its best houses first.

The analysis points to a narrower opening than “another swap site.” A villa exchange wins by solving the villa owner's three specific fears: who is coming, what happens if something breaks, and whether the house coming back is genuinely comparable. That means verified identity, inspected homes, a published points ladder that rewards exceptional houses instead of flattening them, and a human on the phone when a swap goes wrong. The general platforms have scale; a villa platform's edge is curation, and curation is a brand promise before it is a feature list. A name that says villas, and only villas, makes that promise before a visitor reads a word of copy.

The implication for a buyer is timing. The exchange market is consolidating around one giant, yet the giant itself has priced a luxury tier at four times its standard fee — proof that the premium segment wants its own room. The next villa-exchange brand will not out-list HomeExchange. It will out-select it, house by house, on the coasts where villa owners already cluster. The name on that door matters more than usual, because the first thing a villa owner judges is taste. VillaSwaps.com states the trade, the tier, and the standard in two words.
