Regulation Is the New Amenity: The Rental Rules Rewriting the Villa Market

The villa market's scarcest resource in 2026 is not a sea view. It is permission — and the cities that grant it are rewriting what a luxury rental is worth.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Barcelona has a date for the end of its tourist apartments: 2028, when the city plans to let more than 10,000 licensed tourist-apartment permits lapse without renewal. Paris caps eligible primary residences at 90 tourist-rental days a year. Amsterdam has cut annual allowances in selected neighborhoods, and Athens has restricted new registrations in its central districts. These are not the complaints of a single angry council. They are the new operating conditions of the European villa market, and they arrived in the same year the market's own numbers got better.

The regulation has a date and a number. Regulation (EU) 2024/1028 became applicable on May 20, 2026, requiring hosts to hold a verifiable national registration number, platforms to verify it, and activity data to be reported to a single digital entry point in each member state. It does not decide where rentals are legal — the cities do that — but it makes the illegal ones visible for the first time. Spain's own attempt at a national registry collapsed one day earlier, on May 19, 2026, when the Supreme Court annulled the state Single Rental Registry procedure, ruling that the state lacked competence to run a national registry duplicating those the autonomous communities already maintain. The result is a market where the rules are simultaneously harmonized and fragmented: one European framework, seventeen Spanish registries, and a Barcelona landlord who knows the exact year his license dies.

The American side of the market moved in both directions at once. Houston's first short-term-rental ordinance took effect January 1, 2026, with enforcement starting April 1. New York City's Local Law 18 continues to require registration for many operators. In October 2026, three aldermen in New Haven, Connecticut, proposed banning short-term rentals unless an owner or long-term resident runs them — a proposal that would not take effect before March 31, 2027, if it passes. And Indiana went the other way: House Enrolled Act 1210, signed in March 2026 and effective July 1, stops cities and counties from capping the number of rentals, short-term rentals included, while still allowing registration, inspections, and safety rules. The map of American short-term renting is now a patchwork in which the same villa is a business in one state, a violation in one city, and a protected property right in the next.

The market underneath the rules is doing something the headlines miss. Airbnb reported $27.2 billion in gross booking value in the second quarter of 2026, up 16 percent year over year, with revenue of $3.6 billion, up 17 percent, and 148.3 million nights and seats booked, up 10 percent. AirDNA's September 2026 U.S. review found listing growth had slowed to 1.6 percent year over year — the lowest rate since the pandemic — while the average daily rate rose 7.2 percent to $254, revenue per available rental rose 15.3 percent to $136, and September occupancy averaged 53.6 percent, up 3.7 points. AirDNA cut its U.S. supply-growth forecast almost in half between December and July, from 4.6 percent to 2.7 percent for 2026. Europe tells the same story at larger scale: tourists spent 258.8 million nights in EU short-term accommodation booked through Airbnb, Booking, or Expedia in the second quarter of 2026, up 13.1 million nights on the same quarter of 2025. The company is growing. The individual host is not always growing with it: longtime U.S. hosts lost about 13 percent of their winter bookings, according to an AirROI analysis, and peak summer demand held steady afterward.

That split — platform growth against host strain — is where the swap market finds its opening, and the reason is regulatory. A swap is not a rental. No money changes hands for the stay, no nightly rate is charged, and in most jurisdictions the exchange of homes falls outside the registration, night-cap, and licensing regimes built to govern paid stays. HomeExchange charges $235 a year for unlimited exchanges, against thousands per week in rental fees, and its Collection tier for curated luxury homes runs $1,000 a year. The swap does not need to beat the rental market's economics. It needs to be legal in the weeks when renting is not, and available in the cities where renting has been capped out of existence.

Here is the counterargument, and it is the one the regulators will make next. A swap that looks like a rental, books like a rental, and is marketed like a rental will eventually be regulated like one, and the first city to decide that an exchange is a disguised letting will not be the last. The platforms know this: the exchange market, at $1.8 billion to $2.6 billion by the available 2025 counts, is roughly a tenth the size of the luxury villa rental market, and it has stayed small partly because it has stayed beneath notice. Growth brings attention, and attention brings registration forms. The swap's regulatory advantage is real, but it is borrowed time, not a structural moat.

The deeper point is about what the rules are actually pricing. A villa in Barcelona with a license that expires in 2028 is a wasting asset, and its rental income has a visible end date. A villa in a city with a 90-day cap is a part-time business, and its yield has to be calculated on ninety days, not three hundred and sixty-five. A villa in Indiana is a business the state has decided to protect. The regulation is not a footnote to the valuation. It is the valuation, and the owners who have not repriced their properties against the rulebook are carrying last year's numbers into this year's market.

The implication for a buyer is to read the ordinance before the listing. The nightly rate, the occupancy, and the view are all negotiable. The license is not, the cap is not, and the registration number is not. In a market where supply growth has fallen to its weakest rate on record and rates are rising anyway, the scarce thing is not demand. It is the legal right to meet it — and the exchange, which needs no license at all, is the only part of the villa market whose supply cannot be capped by a council vote. That is not a reason to buy a swap platform. It is a reason to understand why one might be worth owning.
